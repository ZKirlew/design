MedPortal: making hospital data readable at a glance

A solo concept for a medical-device customer portal, built from interviews with industry professionals and industry call-log data. This page focuses on the visual side: journey maps, dashboard hierarchy, alert design and a one-page impact infographic.

Role
Solo designer
Type
Concept (incubator idea), not client work
Timeline
May 2026
Tools
Figma, FigJam, Claude
01

The brief

Hospital teams were calling sales reps just to find out where their orders were.

Hospitals depend on device suppliers for equipment, supplies and maintenance, but supplier portals are often organized around the supplier's org chart, not the hospital's tasks. From industry call-log data and interviews with people in the industry, three problems stood out:

01

"I don't know where my order is"

Delays surfaced only when someone called a rep, often after the OR schedule was already affected.

02

"I don't know where to go"

Related information was spread across sections built for different teams.

03

No early warning

Overdue maintenance was found after it affected a procedure, not before.

My job as the designer was to make dense, high-stakes information quick to scan for three very different roles.

02

Journey maps

Three people, one visual template.

I mapped one bad week for each role: John in procurement, Sarah, an OR clinical specialist, and David, a biomedical engineer. All three maps share the same grid: stages across the top, then rows for actions, pain points, touchpoints and an emotion curve, with a final row showing where MedPortal steps in. Because the layout repeats, you can compare the three stories at a glance.

03

Visual hierarchy

The most urgent thing first, then the numbers, then the detail.

Users pick their role during onboarding, and each role gets its own dashboard. All three dashboards follow the same reading order:

  • A plain-language greeting and summary line, like "14 of 16 devices are OR-ready for today."
  • One alert banner for the issue that needs action, with the action button inside it.
  • Large key figures in a single row, each with a short label and a trend.
  • Tables and charts below for detail. Status tags pair a color with a word (Alert, Attention, Ready), so meaning never depends on color alone.
04

Alert design

Calm until it matters.

A system that warns about everything trains people to ignore it. I drew a decision flowchart that sorts each signal by urgency and by whether someone needs to act, giving four tiers: silent, ambient, action needed and critical. Each tier gets its own visual weight, from a quiet timeline update up to a red banner with a pre-drafted action.

Device detail

One score, three reasons

The risk panel turns several data points into one bar and one number, then lists the three factors behind it in plain words. Amber appears 7 days before a device is due; red only appears once it is overdue.

Order detail

A timeline that answers "when did I find out?"

The order timeline marks when the delay was reported, when the team was notified and which OR case it affects, so the impact is visible without a phone call.

05

Impact infographic

Projections, summarized on one page.

To present the concept, I designed a one-page infographic with two side-by-side panels. A light panel shows what changes for the people using the portal, and a dark navy panel shows what changes for the business. Large numerals carry each point, with a one-line explanation beside them. The footer states that these are design targets, not measured results.

These are projections, not results. Figures are informed by industry benchmarks, including Navigant/Fierce Healthcare's hospital supply chain cost analysis, Gartner's 2025 B2B Buying Survey and Spryker/Statista's B2B Self-Service Portal study. They are estimates based on comparable self-service portals, not measured outcomes of this concept.
