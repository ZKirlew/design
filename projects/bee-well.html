Bee Well: a friendly visual language for wellness

A team prototype of a habit-building app for college students, made at the University of Florida. I was lead UI designer and team lead, and I designed the final Store page.

My role
Lead UI designer · team lead
I owned
Deadlines and deliverable reviews, user interviews, the final Store page
Timeline
January – April 2022
Tools
Figma, Miro, Adobe Photoshop
Team
Maclay Ramsey & Kenzie Reneemann (UI), Joseph Addison (Visual), Eric Linger (UX Research)
01

The brief

The college students we interviewed wanted healthier habits, but busy schedules, low motivation and routines that were hard to keep got in the way. In interviews, the things that had helped them before were small wins: crossing off tasks, and calendar and planning apps with a clean, easy UI.

How do you make health goals feel rewarding instead of like another chore?

Our answer was a habit tracker that works like a game: users earn honeycombs for meeting goals and reading health articles, then spend them dressing up their bee.

02

Visual direction

Warm and encouraging, not clinical.

Health apps can feel like a scolding. We wanted Bee Well to feel like a friend cheering you on, so the visual system grew out of one idea: the bee.

A mascot with moods

An illustrated bee whose mood and outfit reflect each user's progress, so progress is something you can see, not just a number.

Honeycomb motif

Hexagons appear in backgrounds, progress shapes and the honeycomb reward, tying the theme to how the app works.

Teal, honey & orange

Honey yellow carries the bee theme, teal frames the screens, and orange appears on the progress and reward cards.

The visual system was a team effort, with Joseph Addison as our visual designer. As lead UI designer and team lead, I kept us on deadline and reviewed each deliverable for accuracy.

03

Grayscale first

Structure before color.

We built the mid-fidelity screens in grayscale, with the bee and the honeycomb pattern already in place. That let us test layout, hierarchy and flow before color could distract anyone.

Iteration · the Store

Giving honeycombs a purpose

Testers asked why they should care about earning honeycombs. Our answer was a Store and inventory where users spend them on accessories for their bee, turning points into a reward you can see.

I designed the final Store page. You can see it on the right of the cover image at the top of this page.

04

Final visuals

Color, illustration and simple charts.

In high fidelity, the teal, honey and orange palette comes in, along with the illustrated bee. Each screen has one clear job.

Onboarding

A welcome survey that sets the tone

Large, friendly answer tiles over the honeycomb pattern ask about goals, barriers and how often users want reminders, and the answers personalize the experience.

Health content

Articles that are easy to scan

Health, Nutrition and Exercise tabs and a search bar keep tips short and easy to find. Reading an article also earns honeycombs.

Progress at a glance

Simple charts, friendly feedback

On the home screen, the bee's level and mood sit above a donut chart of today's goals, a monthly count and a weekly bar chart. Pop-ups celebrate each honeycomb earned.

Goals & Hive

The same system on every screen

Goal lists, the goal form, sharing pop-ups and friends' profiles all reuse the same cards, progress bars and bee illustrations, so the app feels like one product.

05

Looking back

I'm proud of what my team made. With what I've learned since, here's what I would change in the visuals:

  • Check the palette against WCAG contrast before applying it. I now do this from the start, as in Coucou.
  • Add text labels under the navigation icons so they don't rely on icons alone.
  • Make the profile icon smaller so it doesn't compete with the content.
  • Add clearer visual states, like hover and error, and small animations for feedback.
